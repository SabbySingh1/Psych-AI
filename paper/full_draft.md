**Sabadnoor Singh**
Independent Researcher

## Abstract

AI companion platforms respond with unconditional validation, but this has largely been studied within single apps, without a human baseline. Using a unified emotion-classification pipeline applied to four large-scale corpora — ESConv (human counseling, n = 910 conversations), WildChat and LMSYS-Chat-1M (naturalistic and multi-model AI chat, n = 100,000 combined), and ShareChat (public conversations across five commercial platforms, n = 7,936) — this study tested whether AI tracks user distress as human supporters do, and whether this varies by platform. Four converging findings emerged. Users disclosed personal content to AI at roughly one-tenth the rate observed with human counselors (≈10.6×), a gap persisting even in publicly shared AI conversations (d = −0.91 to −1.23). Emotional range within a single AI conversation was four to five standard deviations narrower than with a human counselor. Turn-pair analysis of 177,784 user-to-AI exchanges showed human supporters reduced their own positivity as user distress rose (r = −0.225), while every AI platform showed the opposite direction (r = 0.010–0.106) — a sign inversion, not attenuation. This varied by platform: Gemini's response most closely approximated human tracking; Perplexity's was flattest; Claude's response was flat and non-significant; Grok's was weak but statistically sycophantic. AI-mediated distressed conversations were also markedly shorter than non-distressed ones, complicating engagement-optimization accounts of this behavior. These findings extend recent work on AI companion accommodation (Chu et al., 2025, 2026) beyond single platforms to a systematic, human-baselined, cross-platform comparison, and converge with independent survey and discourse evidence on AI mental-health disclosure and dependency.

## Introduction

Human interaction with conversational AI has moved well past task completion. People bring emotional disclosure, personal reflection, and requests for support into these conversations, often in contexts with no human counterpart in the loop at all. This shift has outpaced the field's understanding of what actually happens, psychologically, on the other side of that exchange. A growing body of work has begun to document specific failure modes — AI companions that validate users unconditionally regardless of context (Chu, Gerard, Pawar, Bickham, & Lerman, 2025, *Illusions of Intimacy: How Emotional Dynamics Shape Human-AI Relationships*), and chat systems that accommodate rather than challenge a user's stated emotional frame even when accommodation is not the appropriate response (Chu, Wu, Chen, Hwang, & Luceri, 2026, *When Chatbots Accommodate: What AI Companions Optimize for in Vulnerable Conversations*). These two studies share a first author and research group (University of Southern California Information Sciences Institute) and represent an active, ongoing line of work on emotional dynamics in AI companion platforms; together they establish that sycophantic, emotionally-flattening behavior exists in AI systems under specific conditions — studied within individual companion apps (Replika, Character.AI), examined in isolation from any human baseline. The present study extends this research program in a direction it has not yet covered: general-purpose, naturalistic AI conversation rather than purpose-built companion apps, compared directly and within a single analysis pipeline against a human counseling baseline.

What has not been established is whether this pattern generalizes: whether it appears consistently across naturalistic, general-purpose AI conversation (not just purpose-built companion apps), whether it varies meaningfully by which commercial platform a person happens to be using, and — critically — how it compares in direction and magnitude to the corresponding behavior of a human support provider engaged in the same basic task. Without a human baseline, "AI responds with stable positive affect" is difficult to interpret: stable positive affect could reflect a design flaw, or it could simply reflect what any calm, professional interlocutor does. Without a cross-platform breakdown, "AI is sycophantic" treats a highly heterogeneous set of systems — different training regimes, different alignment approaches, different intended use cases — as a single undifferentiated condition. And without examining self-disclosure and within-conversation emotional variance alongside response-level mirroring, it is not possible to tell whether any AI sycophancy signature reflects the AI's behavior alone, or a broader pattern in which users bring less emotional range to these conversations in the first place.

This study addresses that gap using four large-scale, naturalistic conversation corpora, analyzed with a single unified emotional-classification and feature-extraction pipeline: ESConv, a dataset of human-to-human emotional support counseling sessions structured around Hill's Helping Skills Theory, used here as a human baseline rather than an AI comparison condition; WildChat, a corpus of real-world, unsolicited ChatGPT conversations; LMSYS-Chat-1M, a multi-model comparison arena; and ShareChat, a corpus of publicly shared conversations spanning five commercial AI platforms (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, and Perplexity), which permits emotional behavior to be examined by platform rather than as a single "AI" category.

The study was originally organized around four research questions, stated directionally prior to analysis:

- **RQ1 (Emotion distribution):** Do AI assistants show a different balance of positive and negative affect than human counselors, independent of the user's own emotional state?
- **RQ2 (Emotional mirroring):** Do human counselors show greater turn-by-turn emotional alignment with the user's stated distress than AI assistants, or do AI systems maintain stable, context-independent affect regardless of what the user brings to the conversation?
- **RQ3 (Self-disclosure asymmetry):** Does the rate at which users disclose personal, emotionally-laden content differ systematically between human-counseling and AI-mediated contexts, and does it vary with the evaluative or naturalistic character of the platform?
- **RQ4 (Emotional arc):** Does a support conversation with a human counselor show a measurable negative-to-positive emotional arc across turns, and does this arc differ from what is observed in AI-mediated conversation?

These four questions motivated the initial dataset selection and pipeline design; they are presented here as the study's organizing research questions rather than as formally pre-registered hypotheses subjected to confirmatory testing, and the Results and Methods sections describe explicitly where the analysis departed from this original four-question structure as early findings prompted additional lines of inquiry (see Methods, Design overview).

This paper's contribution is not the discovery that AI systems can behave sycophantically toward emotional content — that has been shown before, within individual companion platforms. What is new here is the systematic comparison: the same emotional-classification pipeline applied to a human-counseling baseline and three structurally distinct AI-mediated corpora at once; a turn-by-turn mirroring measure computed identically across all of them, including a five-platform breakdown within a single dataset; and two independent converging measures — within-conversation emotional variance and distressed-conversation length — that test whether the sycophancy signature, if present, reflects a narrow response-level artifact or a broader pattern spanning how much emotional range a conversation contains and how it unfolds over time. Precisely because the human baseline and the platform breakdown are analyzed within one unified pipeline rather than compared post hoc across separate studies using different classifiers and different operationalizations, the resulting comparison — where a sycophancy or mirroring-absence signature holds, where it does not, and how strongly it varies by platform — is more directly interpretable than prior single-platform findings allow.

## Methods

### Design overview

This study began as a four-dataset comparison of emotional expression, self-disclosure, and empathy across human counseling and AI-mediated conversation, structured around the four research questions described in the Introduction. The analyses reported under Emotional Valence, Emotional Entropy, Empathy Marker Density, and Self-Disclosure Gap in the Results correspond to that original design. The remaining analyses — the ShareChat platform breakdown, the cross-dataset turn-pair mirroring analysis, within-conversation variance, distressed-conversation behavior, and the crisis-adjacent language analysis — were not specified in advance. They were added after an early finding: the initial comparison of positive valence between ESConv supporters and AI assistants ran in the opposite direction from the original prediction (AI assistants showed markedly *lower* positive valence than human supporters, not higher). That reversal prompted a closer look at mechanism rather than static distribution — specifically, whether AI responses track a user's emotional state turn-by-turn at all, and whether that tracking (or its absence) varies by platform. The turn-pair mirroring analysis, the platform-level breakdown, and the two follow-up robustness checks (within-conversation variance, distressed- and crisis-adjacent-conversation behavior) all followed from that question. This is disclosed here rather than presented as though it were planned from the outset; the exploratory character of these later analyses should be weighed accordingly when interpreting their results, particularly the small-sample crisis-adjacent subset.

A note on internal labeling: the file `hypothesis_results.csv` (Table 2) contains a column of pipeline-internal labels (`H1`–`H5`) assigned by the analysis script when it was first written. These labels correspond to the five statistical tests described under Emotional Valence, Emotional Entropy, Empathy Marker Density, and Emotional Arc in the Results, in the order those tests were implemented in code — they do not correspond to, and should not be read as confirming or disconfirming, the four numbered research questions presented in the Introduction. The two numbering schemes were decoupled after the study's exploratory turn; rather than relabel the underlying output file, that decoupling is noted here explicitly.

**Combined corpus:** 108,846 conversations / 565,804 turns.

LMSYS-Chat-1M is structured as a side-by-side model-response comparison arena; the version of this dataset accessible via the standard schema surfaces only model response turns, with no corresponding user-authored turn retained. As a result, LMSYS contributes zero rows to every analysis conditioned on user turns (self-disclosure, negative valence by user, within-conversation variance, turn-pair mirroring, and the crisis-adjacent flag) — this is a structural property of the source data, not missing or excluded data, and is noted at each point in the Results where it applies.

ShareChat conversations are user-submitted public shares and are self-selected by definition; this is discussed as a limitation below.

### Unified schema and preprocessing

All four datasets were standardized into a common schema: `conversation_id` (a SHA-1 hash of the dataset name and the source-specific conversation identifier, stable across pipeline runs), `dataset`, `turn_number`, `speaker` (`user`, `assistant`, or `supporter` for ESConv's human counselor role), `text`, and `model`. ShareChat additionally retained `platform`, `topic`, and `language` columns. ShareChat was filtered to English-language turns only (`detected_language_final == "English"`) prior to scoring, at up to 10,000 English turns per platform. All other datasets were used in full at the sizes given above.

### Emotion classification

Every turn was scored independently with [SamLowe/roberta-base-go_emotions](https://huggingface.co/SamLowe/roberta-base-go_emotions), a RoBERTa-base model fine-tuned on the Google GoEmotions corpus, producing probabilities across 27 emotion labels plus neutral. Inference was run via direct forward pass (`AutoModelForSequenceClassification`) on Apple Silicon (MPS backend), batch size 128, max sequence length 128 tokens. The 27 GoEmotions labels were additionally collapsed to Ekman's six basic emotions plus neutral, using the mapping in the project README. Two scalar valence scores were derived per turn: `valence_pos` (summed probability mass across GoEmotions labels with clear positive affective valence — admiration, amusement, approval, caring, desire, excitement, gratitude, joy, love, optimism, pride, relief) and `valence_neg` (summed probability mass across negative-valence labels — anger, annoyance, disappointment, disapproval, disgust, embarrassment, fear, grief, nervousness, remorse, sadness). These two scores are not complementary (turns can score low or high on both, or on neither) and were used throughout as separate continuous features rather than a single bipolar scale.

### Linguistic feature extraction

Five lexicon-based turn-level features were computed from raw text using regex tokenization: **self-disclosure** (density of first-person singular pronouns: I, me, my, myself, mine, and contractions); **hedging** (density of an epistemic-uncertainty keyword list: maybe, perhaps, might, seems, unclear, etc.); **empathy markers** (density of a keyword list associated with validating, supportive language: understand, feel, sorry, support, that sounds, makes sense, etc.); **cognitive/analytical style** (density of an analytical-connective keyword list: because, therefore, however, evidence, etc.); and **question rate** (count of `?` characters divided by sentence count, capped at 1.0). Each feature is a simple keyword- or token-density ratio, not a learned or validated psychometric scale, and should be interpreted as a coarse proxy rather than a clinical instrument. **Emotion entropy** was computed per turn as the Shannon entropy (base 2) of the 27-label GoEmotions probability distribution, after clipping to avoid log(0). **Valence arc slope** was computed per conversation as the ordinary-least-squares slope of `valence_pos` (and separately `valence_neg`) regressed on turn number, using centered turn number as the predictor; conversations with fewer than 2 turns were assigned a slope of 0 and excluded from arc-based comparisons where noted.

### Statistical approach

Between-group comparisons used **Kruskal-Wallis H tests** (non-parametric omnibus test across ≥2 groups) followed by pairwise **Mann-Whitney U tests** where the omnibus test was significant. Pairwise p-values were **Bonferroni-corrected** across the full set of pairwise tests reported in a given table (n = 12 for the core cross-dataset comparisons in Table 3). Effect size for each pairwise comparison was the **rank-biserial correlation** (r = 1 − 2U/(n₁n₂)), interpreted using conventional thresholds (small ≥ 0.1, medium ≥ 0.3, large ≥ 0.5, per |r|). For continuous-feature comparisons against the ESConv baseline in the platform-level and crisis-adjacent analyses, **Cohen's d** (pooled SD) was used instead, to match standard reporting conventions in that literature. One-way **ANOVA** (F-test) was used to test whether a categorical user emotional-state variable (distressed / neutral / positive, binned by valence threshold) predicted continuous AI response valence within a single dataset or platform, where the assumption of between-group comparison (rather than a general distributional difference) was the relevant question.

### Turn-pair mirroring construction

For the cross-dataset mirroring analysis, conversations were sorted by `conversation_id` and `turn_number`, and every user turn immediately followed within the same conversation by an assistant or supporter turn was retained as one "turn pair." For each pair, the **Pearson correlation** was computed between the user's `valence_neg` at turn N and the subsequent AI/supporter turn's `valence_pos` (and separately `valence_neg`) at turn N+1, across all pairs within a given dataset or platform. This is a correlational, not causal, design: it measures whether user distress and subsequent AI response valence covary across the corpus, not whether any individual AI turn was generated in response to the preceding user turn's emotional content (which the underlying datasets do not allow verifying directly). Sample sizes per source are reported in Table 7 and ranged from 0 (LMSYS, structurally excluded) to 145,142 (WildChat).

### Distressed-conversation and crisis-adjacent flagging

A conversation was flagged **distressed** if the mean `valence_neg` across all of its user turns exceeded 0.4 (Table 9). A conversation was flagged **crisis-adjacent** if any single user turn contained one of 26 conservative, plain-language phrases associated with hopelessness or giving up (e.g., "can't go on," "not worth living," "nobody would care," "want to disappear"). The phrase list was constructed to capture non-clinical, everyday expressions of acute distress while deliberately excluding any explicit self-harm method language or instructional content; it is a keyword match, not a classifier, and was not validated against any external crisis-language corpus or clinical annotation. It is intended only to identify a plausible highest-stakes subset for aggregate statistical description — it does not diagnose, detect, or make any claim about any individual conversation or user, and false negatives (crisis language not captured by the fixed phrase list) are expected to be substantial given the informality and diversity of naturalistic text. Base rates of flagged conversations were low across all four sources (0.01%–0.66%; Table 10), which limits several of the resulting subset analyses to descriptive rather than inferential conclusions, as noted at each relevant point in the Results.

### Software

All analysis was implemented in Python (pandas, NumPy, SciPy for statistics; Matplotlib for visualization; Hugging Face `transformers` and `datasets` for model inference and data loading). Figures use a validated colorblind-safe categorical palette (adjacent-pair CVD ΔE ≥ 8 in OKLab, per the project's visualization validation pipeline) and are rendered at 300 DPI.

## Results

### Sample

The primary corpus comprised 518,962 scored turns across three datasets — ESConv (910 conversations, 26,648 turns: 12,759 supporter turns, 13,889 user turns), WildChat (50,000 conversations, 290,284 turns, split evenly between user and assistant roles), and LMSYS-Chat-1M (50,000 conversations, 202,030 assistant turns; LMSYS contributes no user-authored turns in this pipeline, as its multi-model comparison structure surfaces only model responses). ShareChat added 46,842 turns across 7,936 conversations spanning five platforms (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, Perplexity), bringing the combined corpus to 565,804 turns. Full descriptive statistics (n, mean, median, SD) for every feature by dataset and speaker role are reported in **Table 1** (`descriptive_stats.csv`).

### Emotional valence and affective tone

Positive valence differed significantly across ESConv supporters, LMSYS assistants, and WildChat assistants (Kruskal-Wallis H = 24,514.73, p < .001). ESConv supporters showed substantially higher mean positive valence (M = 0.554) than either AI condition (LMSYS-assistant M = 0.122; WildChat-assistant M = 0.122), a large effect in both pairwise comparisons (Mann-Whitney U, rank-biserial r = −0.717 and r = −0.648 respectively, both p_adj < .001; **Table 3**). LMSYS and WildChat assistants did not differ meaningfully from one another (r = 0.170, small effect, though still significant after Bonferroni correction at this sample size). Negative valence among users showed the same pattern in reverse: ESConv users expressed substantially more negative affect (M = 0.301) than WildChat users (M = 0.023), a large effect (H = 21,787.96, p < .001; r = −0.757, p_adj < .001). These distributions are shown in **Figure 1**.

### Emotional entropy (variability)

Shannon entropy over the 27-label GoEmotions distribution — an index of emotional range within a turn — differed significantly across ESConv supporters, ESConv users, LMSYS assistants, and WildChat assistants (H = 44,049.08, p < .001; **Figure 2**). ESConv supporters (M = 2.156 bits) and users (M = 1.976 bits) showed markedly higher entropy than either AI assistant condition (LMSYS M = 0.794; WildChat M = 0.726). All four pairwise contrasts were large effects: ESConv-supporter vs. LMSYS-assistant (r = −0.806), ESConv-supporter vs. WildChat-assistant (r = −0.822), ESConv-user vs. LMSYS-assistant (r = −0.715), ESConv-user vs. WildChat-assistant (r = −0.748), all p_adj < .001 (**Table 3**). This was the largest effect size observed among the core cross-dataset comparisons (**Figure 4**, forest plot of all pairwise rank-biserial r values). The full feature-by-group pattern, z-scored across valence, entropy, empathy, self-disclosure, hedging, cognitive style, and question rate, is summarized in the heatmap in **Figure 5**.

### Empathy marker density

Empathy-related keyword density also differed significantly across groups (H = 3,516.20, p < .001). ESConv supporters used empathy markers at nearly an order of magnitude higher density (M = 0.0307) than LMSYS assistants (M = 0.0041, r = −0.217) or WildChat assistants (M = 0.0024, r = −0.208), both small-to-medium effects by conventional r thresholds but highly significant given sample size (p_adj < .001; **Table 3**, **Figure 3**).

### Self-disclosure gap

Self-disclosure — first-person pronoun density in user turns — was highest in ESConv users (M = 0.0945), roughly ten times the rate observed in WildChat users (M = 0.0089; ratio = 10.6×; **Table 1**, **Figure 3**). This gap persisted, though somewhat narrower, even in ShareChat's publicly shared conversations, where self-disclosure across platforms ranged from M = 0.0122 (Perplexity) to M = 0.0299 (Claude) — all approximately one standard deviation below the ESConv-user baseline (Cohen's d ranging from −0.907 for Claude to −1.232 for Perplexity vs. ESConv users; **Table 6**, `sharechat_self_disclosure_by_platform.csv`). Self-disclosure differed significantly across the five ShareChat platforms (Kruskal-Wallis H = 718.14, p < .001), with Claude eliciting the highest disclosure and Perplexity the lowest. **Figure 8** displays self-disclosure across all sources (ESConv, WildChat, LMSYS, and the five ShareChat platforms) with 95% confidence intervals.

### Within-conversation emotional variance

Within-conversation standard deviation of user negative valence — an index of how much a user's emotional expression moves across a single conversation, independent of its mean level — differed enormously between ESConv and the AI-mediated datasets (**Table 8**, `within_conversation_variance.csv`). ESConv conversations (n = 910) showed a mean within-conversation SD of 0.392; WildChat conversations (n = 50,000) showed a mean of 0.010 (Cohen's d = −5.26 vs. ESConv); ShareChat conversations with at least two user turns (n = 7,727) showed a mean of 0.017 (d = −4.75 vs. ESConv). LMSYS is not represented in this analysis, as it contains no user-authored turns. These are among the largest effect sizes reported in this study.

### Emotional arc across the conversation

The linear slope of positive valence across turn number, computed per conversation, differed significantly across ESConv, LMSYS, and WildChat (H = 1,091.66, p = 8.91 × 10⁻²³⁸; **Table 2**). Contrary to the pattern observed on static valence, LMSYS conversations showed the steepest positive arc slope (M = 0.0785), followed by ShareChat (M = 0.0432, not included in the omnibus test but shown descriptively), WildChat (M = 0.0389), and ESConv (M = 0.0295) — the lowest of the four (**Figure 6**). ESConv's arc slope differed significantly from both LMSYS (r = −0.337, medium effect, p_adj = 4.30 × 10⁻⁶⁷) and WildChat (r = −0.540, large effect, p_adj = 3.24 × 10⁻¹⁷¹). Because a single linear slope can obscure where in the conversation this movement occurs, positive valence was also computed by conversation quintile (**Figure 11**). ESConv showed a steep, steadily increasing trajectory across the conversation (quintile 1 M ≈ 0.25 to quintile 5 M ≈ 0.87). WildChat, LMSYS, and ShareChat all remained comparatively flat throughout, in the M ≈ 0.08–0.25 range across all five quintiles, with ShareChat showing the mildest upward drift of the three AI sources.

### Platform-level differences within ShareChat

Within ShareChat, AI assistant turns differed significantly across the five platforms on positive valence (H = 1,226.99, p = 2.24 × 10⁻²⁶⁴), emotion entropy (H = 1,028.86, p = 1.98 × 10⁻²²¹), and empathy marker density (H = 545.98, p = 7.59 × 10⁻¹¹⁷; **Table 4**, `sharechat_platform_anova.csv`). Relative to the ESConv-supporter baseline, all five platforms showed large negative effect sizes on valence, entropy, and empathy, but the magnitude varied substantially by platform. Gemini showed the smallest gap from the human baseline on valence (d = −0.549) and entropy (d = −1.377); Perplexity showed the largest gap on all three measures (valence d = −1.141; entropy d = −2.316; empathy d = −0.643). ChatGPT, Claude, and Grok fell between these two poles. This ordering is displayed in **Figure 10**.

When restricted to user turns expressing high distress (valence_neg > 0.4), AI response valence differed significantly by platform (Kruskal-Wallis H = 66.95, p = 1.00 × 10⁻¹³; **Table 5**, `sharechat_mirroring_by_platform.csv`). Gemini produced the warmest response to distressed users (mean AI valence_pos = 0.452, mean empathy = 0.0196, n = 241 distressed turns), while Perplexity produced the flattest (mean AI valence_pos = 0.119, mean empathy = 0.0054, n = 46 distressed turns). ChatGPT, Claude, and Grok again fell between these two poles.

### Cross-dataset turn-pair emotional mirroring

The central analysis of this study examined, for every consecutive user-turn-then-AI-turn pair (n = 177,784 pairs across all sources), the Pearson correlation between user negative valence at turn N and the subsequent assistant response's positive valence at turn N+1 (**Table 7**, `mirroring_coefficients.csv`; **Figures 7 and 9**). In ESConv, human supporters showed a negative correlation between user distress and their own subsequent positivity (r = −0.225, n = 10,191 pairs, p < .001) alongside a positive correlation between user distress and their own subsequent negative-valence expression (r = 0.298, p < .001) — a pattern consistent with supporters reducing positivity and increasing negative-affect language as users express more distress. Every AI-mediated source showed the opposite sign on the positivity correlation: WildChat (r = 0.097, n = 145,142, p < .001), ShareChat overall (r = 0.082, n = 22,451, p < .001), and each ShareChat platform individually — ChatGPT (r = 0.080, p < .001), Gemini (r = 0.106, p < .001), Grok (r = 0.038, p = .009), Claude (r = 0.027, p = .123, not significant), and Perplexity (r = 0.010, p = .496, not significant). One-way ANOVAs confirmed that user emotional state (distressed / neutral / positive, binned by valence thresholds) significantly predicted AI response valence in every AI-mediated source tested (all F > 65, all p < .001). LMSYS is not represented in this analysis (0 eligible turn pairs), as it contains no user-authored turns and is therefore structurally excluded from any turn-pair mirroring measure. **Figure 9** contrasts ESConv and WildChat directly on both correlation directions (positivity and negativity response), and **Figure 7** displays the positivity-response coefficient across all eight sources with ESConv as the sole negative bar.

### Distressed-conversation behavior

Conversations were flagged as distressed if the mean negative valence across all user turns exceeded 0.4 (**Table 9**, `distressed_conversations.csv`). By this criterion, 20.99% of ESConv conversations (191 of 910) qualified as distressed, compared with 0.71% of WildChat conversations (≈355 of 50,000) and 1.11% of ShareChat conversations (≈86 of 7,727 conversations with ≥1 user turn). In ESConv, mean AI-analog (supporter) positive valence was slightly *lower* in distressed conversations (M = 0.528) than non-distressed conversations (M = 0.567). In WildChat and ShareChat, the pattern reversed: mean AI positive valence was *higher* in distressed conversations (WildChat: 0.279 distressed vs. 0.130 non-distressed; ShareChat: 0.223 distressed vs. 0.138 non-distressed). Conversation length also diverged: ESConv distressed conversations were marginally shorter than non-distressed ones (25.24 vs. 29.09 turns), while WildChat and ShareChat distressed conversations were substantially shorter than their non-distressed counterparts (WildChat: 2.01 vs. 4.83 turns; ShareChat: 4.15 vs. 6.77 turns).

### Crisis-adjacent language analysis

A conservative keyword flag (26 plain-language phrases indicating hopelessness or giving up, deliberately excluding any explicit self-harm method language; see Methods) was applied to all user turns to identify conversations containing crisis-adjacent language (**Table 10**, `crisis_adjacent_analysis.csv`; **Table 11**, `crisis_adjacent_mirroring.csv`). The base rate of flagged language was low across all sources: 6 of 910 ESConv conversations (0.66%), 19 of 50,000 WildChat conversations (0.04%), 0 of 50,000 LMSYS conversations, and 1 of 7,936 ShareChat conversations (0.01%).

Within flagged ESConv conversations (n = 72 eligible turn pairs), the mirroring pattern reported above intensified rather than attenuated: the correlation between user distress and subsequent supporter positivity strengthened to r = −0.390 (p < .001), and the correlation with subsequent supporter negative-valence expression strengthened to r = 0.546 (p < .001), both stronger than the full-sample ESConv coefficients. Within flagged WildChat conversations (n = 47 eligible turn pairs), the positivity correlation was directionally consistent with the sycophancy pattern reported above but did not reach significance (r = 0.065, p = .665). Flagged ShareChat conversations yielded only 10 eligible turn pairs; the negativity correlation reached significance (r = 0.640, p = .046) but the positivity correlation did not (r = −0.123, p = .734). No flagged conversations were available in LMSYS. Given the small number of flagged conversations in every AI-mediated source (WildChat n = 19, ShareChat n = 1, LMSYS n = 0), these subset analyses are underpowered to test the AI-side hypothesis formally and are reported as directionally suggestive rather than confirmatory; the ESConv result, by contrast, was estimated from a larger pair count and reached conventional significance.

## Discussion

### A four-part mechanism story

Taken together, the results in this study describe a coherent chain rather than four unrelated findings. First, users disclose personal, emotionally-laden content to AI systems at roughly one-tenth the rate they disclose to a human counselor (self-disclosure M = 0.0945 in ESConv users vs. M = 0.0089 in WildChat users), and this gap persists — narrower but still large (Cohen's d = −0.91 to −1.23) — even in ShareChat's publicly shared conversations, where users have already chosen to make the exchange visible to others. Second, the emotional range within a single conversation is far narrower with AI: within-conversation variance in user negative valence was roughly four to five standard deviations lower in WildChat and ShareChat than in ESConv. Third, when users do bring measurable distress into an AI conversation, the AI's response moves in the opposite direction from what a human supporter's does — every AI-mediated source in this study showed a positive correlation between user distress and the AI's subsequent positive-valence language, while ESConv showed a negative correlation of similar magnitude. Fourth, distressed conversations with AI are shorter, not longer, than non-distressed ones, in contrast to the pattern in ESConv, where distressed conversations are only marginally shorter than non-distressed ones and remain substantially longer in absolute terms (25.24 vs. 2.01–4.15 turns).

Each of these four findings, alone, would be a modest empirical contribution. Together, they describe a plausible mechanism: people bring a narrower emotional register to AI conversations in the first place, and when they do occasionally surface something closer to real distress, the system's affective response does not track it the way a trained human supporter's does — and the conversation ends sooner rather than continuing to work through it. This paper does not have longitudinal or causal evidence establishing that this pattern *shapes* user behavior over time (a limitation discussed below); the cross-sectional turn-pair design tested here establishes that this pattern exists as a general property of the corpora examined, not that it causes any particular downstream outcome.

### The platform hierarchy and its design implications

The platform-level comparison within ShareChat showed that this pattern is not uniform across commercial AI systems. Gemini consistently showed the response closest to the ESConv baseline across three independent measures — the smallest valence gap from the human baseline (Cohen's d = −0.549), the warmest response to distressed users (mean AI valence_pos = 0.452 in distressed turns), and the strongest sycophancy-direction mirroring coefficient among the platforms that reached significance (r = 0.106). Perplexity showed the opposite pattern on the first two measures — the largest valence gap (d = −1.141) and the flattest response to distress (mean AI valence_pos = 0.119) — while showing the weakest, non-significant mirroring coefficient (r = 0.010, p = .496). Claude and Grok fell in between and were classified as flat rather than sycophantic under the magnitude threshold (r < 0.05) used to categorize platform responses in this study; this classification should be read with its statistical basis in mind, since it is not equivalent to non-significance. Claude's positivity correlation did not reach significance (r = 0.027, p = .123), consistent with a genuinely flat response; Grok's positivity correlation, though small, was itself statistically significant (r = 0.038, p = .009), and both platforms' negativity correlations were significant (Claude r = 0.214, p < .001; Grok r = 0.221, p < .001). A purely flat, distress-independent response pattern is therefore better supported for Claude than for Grok, whose response is weak in magnitude but still reliably distress-dependent — distinct from Gemini's warmer, more strongly sycophantic profile and from ESConv's true mirroring profile.

This convergence across three independently computed measures — static valence gap, response-to-distress warmth, and turn-pair correlation — makes it unlikely that the platform ordering is an artifact of any single metric's construction. It suggests that the degree to which a system tracks or ignores user distress is a measurable, platform-specific design property, plausibly shaped by differences in alignment training, system prompting, or product positioning (a conversational assistant marketed for search and factual retrieval, such as Perplexity, may be tuned toward flat, non-committal affect by design, in a way a general-purpose conversational assistant is not). This has a direct design implication: emotional responsiveness to user distress is not a fixed property of "AI chatbots" as a category, and evaluating or regulating this behavior at the level of an undifferentiated AI category will miss meaningful, measurable variation between systems that users can and do choose between.

### Real-world grounding

The disclosure and mirroring-inversion findings reported here converge with two independent sources of evidence outside this study's own data. Zhu, Coifman, and Jin (2026) analyzed user-authored discourse describing difficulty regulating and limiting AI chatbot use, and reported first-person accounts consistent with a pattern in which the AI's consistent responsiveness and lack of pushback made disengagement harder rather than easier — a qualitative, self-report account of the same asymmetry this study measures behaviorally: an AI that responds with stable or increasing warmth regardless of what a user brings to it. Separately, McBain et al. (2026), in a nationally representative survey published in *JAMA Pediatrics*, reported that a substantial share of US adolescents and young adults who use AI chatbots for mental health purposes disclose this use to no one, including their own therapist. Read alongside the present findings, this suggests a compounding pattern rather than two independent facts: reduced social visibility of AI use for emotional purposes, paired with behavioral evidence that the AI system receiving that disclosure does not track the user's distress the way a human support provider does. Neither this study nor the two cited above establishes a causal link between the behavioral pattern and the disclosure pattern; the convergence is presented here as consistent, complementary evidence from independent methodologies (behavioral corpus analysis, self-report discourse analysis, and survey epidemiology) rather than as a single demonstrated causal chain.

The present findings also complicate, rather than confirm, one common framing in AI-criticism discourse — the idea that sycophantic AI behavior functions straightforwardly as an engagement-optimization tactic, keeping users in longer conversations by agreeing with them. The distressed-conversation length finding runs the opposite direction: AI-mediated distressed conversations were markedly *shorter* than non-distressed ones (WildChat: 2.01 vs. 4.83 turns; ShareChat: 4.15 vs. 6.77 turns), not longer. Whatever is driving the sycophancy-direction mirroring signature observed here, it does not appear to be operating through simple conversation-length engagement optimization, at least not as measured in these corpora — a finding that should inform, rather than be absorbed into, existing critical narratives about AI engagement design.

### Limitations

Several limitations bound the interpretation of these results. The design is cross-sectional and correlational throughout: the turn-pair mirroring measure establishes that user distress and subsequent AI valence covary across the corpus, not that any specific AI turn was causally shaped by the emotional content of the immediately preceding user turn, nor that this pattern would replicate in a controlled, prompted comparison. All analyses are restricted to English-language text, using a single emotion classifier (GoEmotions RoBERTa) trained on Reddit comments, a domain that differs in register and context from emotional-support counseling transcripts, general AI chat, and publicly shared conversations alike; classifier domain mismatch could inflate or deflate valence and entropy estimates unevenly across datasets in ways not fully separable from genuine between-dataset differences. The crisis-adjacent language flag is a conservative, unvalidated keyword match, not a clinical or machine-learned classifier, and its low base rate across all four sources (0.01%–0.66% of conversations) left every AI-mediated subset analysis underpowered; the ESConv-side intensification finding in that subset (n = 72 turn pairs) should be treated as suggestive pending replication in a larger crisis-language corpus, and the corresponding AI-side null results (WildChat n = 47 pairs, ShareChat n = 10 pairs, LMSYS n = 0) should not be read as evidence of absence. ShareChat conversations are self-selected: users chose to make them public, which plausibly biases this subset toward more socially acceptable or shareable content and away from the most personally sensitive exchanges, in a direction that would tend to understate rather than overstate the disclosure gap and mirroring inversion reported here. Finally, LMSYS's structural absence of user-authored turns removes it entirely from four of the study's analyses (self-disclosure, within-conversation variance, turn-pair mirroring, and the crisis-adjacent flag); the platform-level comparisons involving ShareChat's five sub-platforms, while internally consistent, are not independently replicated in a second multi-platform corpus.

### Implications for AI design and mental health policy

The convergence of three independent measures on a stable platform ordering (Gemini closest to the human baseline; Perplexity furthest; Claude non-significant and Grok weak-but-significant on the mirroring measure) suggests that emotional responsiveness to distress is a tractable design target, not an unavoidable property of large language models generally. For AI product design, this argues for treating distress-responsiveness as a measurable, testable property subject to the same kind of pre-deployment evaluation already applied to safety and factuality, rather than an emergent side effect to be addressed only after harm is reported. For mental health policy and clinical practice, the combination of the low-disclosure finding here and the non-disclosure-to-clinicians finding in McBain et al. (2026) suggests that AI-mediated emotional disclosure is likely to remain largely invisible to the support systems — therapists, counselors, caregivers — that would otherwise be positioned to notice and respond to genuine distress. Given that this study's own crisis-adjacent analysis was underpowered specifically because crisis-level language is rare in these corpora, an important direction for future work is a larger-scale, purpose-built study of AI conversations in which acute distress is confirmed present, using either clinically-validated crisis-language detection or prospective, consented data collection with distressed users — a study this corpus, by design, is not equipped to provide.
