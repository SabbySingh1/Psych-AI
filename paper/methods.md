## Methods

### Design overview

This study began as a four-dataset comparison of emotional expression, self-disclosure, and empathy across human counseling and AI-mediated conversation, structured around the four research questions described in the Introduction. The analyses reported under Emotional Valence, Emotional Entropy, Empathy Marker Density, and Self-Disclosure Gap in the Results correspond to that original design. The remaining analyses — the ShareChat platform breakdown, the cross-dataset turn-pair mirroring analysis, within-conversation variance, distressed-conversation behavior, and the crisis-adjacent language analysis — were not specified in advance. They were added after an early finding: the initial comparison of positive valence between ESConv supporters and AI assistants ran in the opposite direction from the original prediction (AI assistants showed markedly *lower* positive valence than human supporters, not higher). That reversal prompted a closer look at mechanism rather than static distribution — specifically, whether AI responses track a user's emotional state turn-by-turn at all, and whether that tracking (or its absence) varies by platform. The turn-pair mirroring analysis, the platform-level breakdown, and the two follow-up robustness checks (within-conversation variance, distressed- and crisis-adjacent-conversation behavior) all followed from that question. This is disclosed here rather than presented as though it were planned from the outset; the exploratory character of these later analyses should be weighed accordingly when interpreting their results, particularly the small-sample crisis-adjacent subset.

A note on internal labeling: the file `hypothesis_results.csv` (Table 2) contains a column of pipeline-internal labels (`H1`–`H5`) assigned by the analysis script when it was first written. These labels correspond to the five statistical tests described under Emotional Valence, Emotional Entropy, Empathy Marker Density, and Emotional Arc in the Results, in the order those tests were implemented in code — they do not correspond to, and should not be read as confirming or disconfirming, the four numbered research questions presented in the Introduction. The two numbering schemes were decoupled after the study's exploratory turn; rather than relabel the underlying output file, that decoupling is noted here explicitly.

### Datasets

| Dataset | Conversations | Turns | Language | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [ESConv](https://huggingface.co/datasets/thu-coai/esconv) | 910 | 26,648 | English | Human-to-human emotional support counseling |
| [WildChat-1M](https://huggingface.co/datasets/allenai/WildChat-1M) | 50,000 | 290,284 | English | Real-world ChatGPT interaction logs |
| [LMSYS-Chat-1M](https://huggingface.co/datasets/lmsys/lmsys-chat-1m) | 50,000 | 202,030 | English | Multi-model chat arena (gated; assistant turns only) |
| [ShareChat](https://huggingface.co/datasets/tucnguyen/ShareChat) | 7,936 | 46,842 | English | Publicly shared conversations across 5 commercial AI platforms (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, Perplexity) |

**Combined corpus:** 108,846 conversations / 565,804 turns.

LMSYS-Chat-1M is structured as a side-by-side model-response comparison arena; the version of this dataset accessible via the standard schema surfaces only model response turns, with no corresponding user-authored turn retained. As a result, LMSYS contributes zero rows to every analysis conditioned on user turns (self-disclosure, negative valence by user, within-conversation variance, turn-pair mirroring, and the crisis-adjacent flag) — this is a structural property of the source data, not missing or excluded data, and is noted at each point in the Results where it applies.

ShareChat conversations are user-submitted public shares and are self-selected by definition; this is discussed as a limitation below.

### Unified schema and preprocessing

All four datasets were standardized into a common schema: `conversation_id` (a SHA-1 hash of the dataset name and the source-specific conversation identifier, stable across pipeline runs), `dataset`, `turn_number`, `speaker` (`user`, `assistant`, or `supporter` for ESConv's human counselor role), `text`, and `model`. ShareChat additionally retained `platform`, `topic`, and `language` columns. ShareChat was filtered to English-language turns only (`detected_language_final == "English"`) prior to scoring, at up to 10,000 English turns per platform. All other datasets were used in full at the sizes given above.

### Emotion classification

Every turn was scored independently with [SamLowe/roberta-base-go_emotions](https://huggingface.co/SamLowe/roberta-base-go_emotions), a RoBERTa-base model fine-tuned on the Google GoEmotions corpus, producing probabilities across 27 emotion labels plus neutral. Inference was run via direct forward pass (`AutoModelForSequenceClassification`) on Apple Silicon (MPS backend), batch size 128, max sequence length 128 tokens. The 27 GoEmotions labels were additionally collapsed to Ekman's six basic emotions plus neutral, using the mapping in the project README. Two scalar valence scores were derived per turn: `valence_pos` (summed probability mass across GoEmotions labels with clear positive affective valence — admiration, amusement, approval, caring, desire, excitement, gratitude, joy, love, optimism, pride, relief) and `valence_neg` (summed probability mass across negative-valence labels — anger, annoyance, disappointment, disapproval, disgust, embarrassment, fear, grief, nervousness, remorse, sadness). These two scores are not complementary (turns can score low or high on both, or on neither) and were used throughout as separate continuous features rather than a single bipolar scale.

### Linguistic feature extraction

Five lexicon-based turn-level features were computed from raw text using regex tokenization: **self-disclosure** (density of first-person singular pronouns: I, me, my, myself, mine, and contractions); **hedging** (density of an epistemic-uncertainty keyword list: maybe, perhaps, might, seems, unclear, etc.); **empathy markers** (density of a keyword list associated with validating, supportive language: understand, feel, sorry, support, that sounds, makes sense, etc.); **cognitive/analytical style** (density of an analytical-connective keyword list: because, therefore, however, evidence, etc.); and **question rate** (count of `?` characters divided by sentence count, capped at 1.0). Each feature is a simple keyword- or token-density ratio, not a learned or validated psychometric scale, and should be interpreted as a coarse proxy rather than a clinical instrument. **Emotion entropy** was computed per turn as the Shannon entropy (base 2) of the 27-label GoEmotions probability distribution, after clipping to avoid log(0). **Valence arc slope** was computed per conversation as the ordinary-least-squares slope of `valence_pos` (and separately `valence_neg`) regressed on turn number, using centered turn number as the predictor; conversations with fewer than 2 turns were assigned a slope of 0 and excluded from arc-based comparisons where noted.

### Statistical approach

Between-group comparisons used **Kruskal-Wallis H tests** (non-parametric omnibus test across ≥2 groups) followed by pairwise **Mann-Whitney U tests** where the omnibus test was significant. Pairwise p-values were **Bonferroni-corrected** across the full set of pairwise tests reported in a given table (n = 12 for the core cross-dataset comparisons in Table 3). Effect size for each pairwise comparison was the **rank-biserial correlation** (r = 1 − 2U/(n₁n₂)), interpreted using conventional thresholds (small ≥ 0.1, medium ≥ 0.3, large ≥ 0.5, per |r|). For continuous-feature comparisons against the ESConv baseline in the platform-level and crisis-adjacent analyses, **Cohen's d** (pooled SD) was used instead, to match standard reporting conventions in that literature. One-way **ANOVA** (F-test) was used to test whether a categorical user emotional-state variable (distressed / neutral / positive, binned by valence threshold) predicted continuous AI response valence within a single dataset or platform, where the assumption of between-group comparison (rather than a general distributional difference) was the relevant question.

### Turn-pair mirroring construction

For the cross-dataset mirroring analysis, conversations were sorted by `conversation_id` and `turn_number`, and every user turn immediately followed within the same conversation by an assistant or supporter turn was retained as one "turn pair." For each pair, the **Pearson correlation** was computed between the user's `valence_neg` at turn N and the subsequent AI/supporter turn's `valence_pos` (and separately `valence_neg`) at turn N+1, across all pairs within a given dataset or platform. This is a correlational, not causal, design: it measures whether user distress and subsequent AI response valence covary across the corpus, not whether any individual AI turn was generated in response to the preceding user turn's emotional content (which the underlying datasets do not allow verifying directly). Sample sizes per source are reported in Table 7 and ranged from 0 (LMSYS, structurally excluded) to 145,142 (WildChat).

### Distressed-conversation and crisis-adjacent flagging

A conversation was flagged **distressed** if the mean `valence_neg` across all of its user turns exceeded 0.4 (Table 9). A conversation was flagged **crisis-adjacent** if any single user turn contained one of 26 conservative, plain-language phrases associated with hopelessness or giving up (e.g., "can't go on," "not worth living," "nobody would care," "want to disappear"). The phrase list was constructed to capture non-clinical, everyday expressions of acute distress while deliberately excluding any explicit self-harm method language or instructional content; it is a keyword match, not a classifier, and was not validated against any external crisis-language corpus or clinical annotation. It is intended only to identify a plausible highest-stakes subset for aggregate statistical description — it does not diagnose, detect, or make any claim about any individual conversation or user, and false negatives (crisis language not captured by the fixed phrase list) are expected to be substantial given the informality and diversity of naturalistic text. Base rates of flagged conversations were low across all four sources (0.01%–0.66%; Table 10), which limits several of the resulting subset analyses to descriptive rather than inferential conclusions, as noted at each relevant point in the Results.

### Software

All analysis was implemented in Python (pandas, NumPy, SciPy for statistics; Matplotlib for visualization; Hugging Face `transformers` and `datasets` for model inference and data loading). Figures use a validated colorblind-safe categorical palette (adjacent-pair CVD ΔE ≥ 8 in OKLab, per the project's visualization validation pipeline) and are rendered at 300 DPI.
