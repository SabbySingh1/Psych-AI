## Introduction

Human interaction with conversational AI has moved well past task completion. People bring emotional disclosure, personal reflection, and requests for support into these conversations, often in contexts with no human counterpart in the loop at all. This shift has outpaced the field's understanding of what actually happens, psychologically, on the other side of that exchange. A growing body of work has begun to document specific failure modes — AI companions that validate users unconditionally regardless of context (Chu, Gerard, Pawar, Bickham, & Lerman, 2025, *Illusions of Intimacy: How Emotional Dynamics Shape Human-AI Relationships*), and chat systems that accommodate rather than challenge a user's stated emotional frame even when accommodation is not the appropriate response (Chu, Wu, Chen, Hwang, & Luceri, 2026, *When Chatbots Accommodate: What AI Companions Optimize for in Vulnerable Conversations*). These two studies share a first author and research group (University of Southern California Information Sciences Institute) and represent an active, ongoing line of work on emotional dynamics in AI companion platforms; together they establish that sycophantic, emotionally-flattening behavior exists in AI systems under specific conditions — studied within individual companion apps (Replika, Character.AI), examined in isolation from any human baseline. The present study extends this research program in a direction it has not yet covered: general-purpose, naturalistic AI conversation rather than purpose-built companion apps, compared directly and within a single analysis pipeline against a human counseling baseline.

What has not been established is whether this pattern generalizes: whether it appears consistently across naturalistic, general-purpose AI conversation (not just purpose-built companion apps), whether it varies meaningfully by which commercial platform a person happens to be using, and — critically — how it compares in direction and magnitude to the corresponding behavior of a human support provider engaged in the same basic task. Without a human baseline, "AI responds with stable positive affect" is difficult to interpret: stable positive affect could reflect a design flaw, or it could simply reflect what any calm, professional interlocutor does. Without a cross-platform breakdown, "AI is sycophantic" treats a highly heterogeneous set of systems — different training regimes, different alignment approaches, different intended use cases — as a single undifferentiated condition. And without examining self-disclosure and within-conversation emotional variance alongside response-level mirroring, it is not possible to tell whether any AI sycophancy signature reflects the AI's behavior alone, or a broader pattern in which users bring less emotional range to these conversations in the first place.

This study addresses that gap using four large-scale, naturalistic conversation corpora, analyzed with a single unified emotional-classification and feature-extraction pipeline: ESConv, a dataset of human-to-human emotional support counseling sessions structured around Hill's Helping Skills Theory, used here as a human baseline rather than an AI comparison condition; WildChat, a corpus of real-world, unsolicited ChatGPT conversations; LMSYS-Chat-1M, a multi-model comparison arena; and ShareChat, a corpus of publicly shared conversations spanning five commercial AI platforms (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, and Perplexity), which permits emotional behavior to be examined by platform rather than as a single "AI" category.

The study was originally organized around four research questions, stated directionally prior to analysis:

- **RQ1 (Emotion distribution):** Do AI assistants show a different balance of positive and negative affect than human counselors, independent of the user's own emotional state?
- **RQ2 (Emotional mirroring):** Do human counselors show greater turn-by-turn emotional alignment with the user's stated distress than AI assistants, or do AI systems maintain stable, context-independent affect regardless of what the user brings to the conversation?
- **RQ3 (Self-disclosure asymmetry):** Does the rate at which users disclose personal, emotionally-laden content differ systematically between human-counseling and AI-mediated contexts, and does it vary with the evaluative or naturalistic character of the platform?
- **RQ4 (Emotional arc):** Does a support conversation with a human counselor show a measurable negative-to-positive emotional arc across turns, and does this arc differ from what is observed in AI-mediated conversation?

These four questions motivated the initial dataset selection and pipeline design; they are presented here as the study's organizing research questions rather than as formally pre-registered hypotheses subjected to confirmatory testing, and the Results and Methods sections describe explicitly where the analysis departed from this original four-question structure as early findings prompted additional lines of inquiry (see Methods, Design overview).

This paper's contribution is not the discovery that AI systems can behave sycophantically toward emotional content — that has been shown before, within individual companion platforms. What is new here is the systematic comparison: the same emotional-classification pipeline applied to a human-counseling baseline and three structurally distinct AI-mediated corpora at once; a turn-by-turn mirroring measure computed identically across all of them, including a five-platform breakdown within a single dataset; and two independent converging measures — within-conversation emotional variance and distressed-conversation length — that test whether the sycophancy signature, if present, reflects a narrow response-level artifact or a broader pattern spanning how much emotional range a conversation contains and how it unfolds over time. Precisely because the human baseline and the platform breakdown are analyzed within one unified pipeline rather than compared post hoc across separate studies using different classifiers and different operationalizations, the resulting comparison — where a sycophancy or mirroring-absence signature holds, where it does not, and how strongly it varies by platform — is more directly interpretable than prior single-platform findings allow.
