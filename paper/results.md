## Results

### Sample

The primary corpus comprised 518,962 scored turns across three datasets — ESConv (910 conversations, 26,648 turns: 12,759 supporter turns, 13,889 user turns), WildChat (50,000 conversations, 290,284 turns, split evenly between user and assistant roles), and LMSYS-Chat-1M (50,000 conversations, 202,030 assistant turns; LMSYS contributes no user-authored turns in this pipeline, as its multi-model comparison structure surfaces only model responses). ShareChat added 46,842 turns across 7,936 conversations spanning five platforms (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, Perplexity), bringing the combined corpus to 565,804 turns. Full descriptive statistics (n, mean, median, SD) for every feature by dataset and speaker role are reported in **Table 1** (`descriptive_stats.csv`).

### Emotional valence and affective tone

Positive valence differed significantly across ESConv supporters, LMSYS assistants, and WildChat assistants (Kruskal-Wallis H = 24,514.73, p < .001). ESConv supporters showed substantially higher mean positive valence (M = 0.554) than either AI condition (LMSYS-assistant M = 0.122; WildChat-assistant M = 0.122), a large effect in both pairwise comparisons (Mann-Whitney U, rank-biserial r = −0.717 and r = −0.648 respectively, both p_adj < .001; **Table 3**). LMSYS and WildChat assistants did not differ meaningfully from one another (r = 0.170, small effect, though still significant after Bonferroni correction at this sample size). Negative valence among users showed the same pattern in reverse: ESConv users expressed substantially more negative affect (M = 0.301) than WildChat users (M = 0.023), a large effect (H = 21,787.96, p < .001; r = −0.757, p_adj < .001). These distributions are shown in **Figure 1**.

### Emotional entropy (variability)

Shannon entropy over the 27-label GoEmotions distribution — an index of emotional range within a turn — differed significantly across ESConv supporters, ESConv users, LMSYS assistants, and WildChat assistants (H = 44,049.08, p < .001; **Figure 2**). ESConv supporters (M = 2.156 bits) and users (M = 1.976 bits) showed markedly higher entropy than either AI assistant condition (LMSYS M = 0.794; WildChat M = 0.726). All four pairwise contrasts were large effects: ESConv-supporter vs. LMSYS-assistant (r = −0.806), ESConv-supporter vs. WildChat-assistant (r = −0.822), ESConv-user vs. LMSYS-assistant (r = −0.715), ESConv-user vs. WildChat-assistant (r = −0.748), all p_adj < .001 (**Table 3**). This was the largest effect size observed among the core cross-dataset comparisons (**Figure 4**, forest plot of all pairwise rank-biserial r values). The full feature-by-group pattern, z-scored across valence, entropy, empathy, self-disclosure, hedging, cognitive style, and question rate, is summarized in the heatmap in **Figure 5**.

### Empathy marker density

Empathy-related keyword density also differed significantly across groups (H = 3,516.20, p < .001). ESConv supporters used empathy markers at nearly an order of magnitude higher density (M = 0.0307) than LMSYS assistants (M = 0.0041, r = −0.217) or WildChat assistants (M = 0.0024, r = −0.208), both small-to-medium effects by conventional r thresholds but highly significant given sample size (p_adj < .001; **Table 3**, **Figure 3**).

### Self-disclosure gap

Self-disclosure — first-person pronoun density in user turns — was highest in ESConv users (M = 0.0945), roughly ten times the rate observed in WildChat users (M = 0.0089; ratio = 10.6×; **Table 1**, **Figure 3**). This gap persisted, though somewhat narrower, even in ShareChat's publicly shared conversations, where self-disclosure across platforms ranged from M = 0.0122 (Perplexity) to M = 0.0299 (Claude) — all approximately one standard deviation below the ESConv-user baseline (Cohen's d ranging from −0.907 for Claude to −1.232 for Perplexity vs. ESConv users; **Table 6**, `sharechat_self_disclosure_by_platform.csv`). Self-disclosure differed significantly across the five ShareChat platforms (Kruskal-Wallis H = 718.14, p < .001), with Claude eliciting the highest disclosure and Perplexity the lowest. **Figure 8** displays self-disclosure across all sources (ESConv, WildChat, LMSYS, and the five ShareChat platforms) with 95% confidence intervals.

### Within-conversation emotional variance

Within-conversation standard deviation of user negative valence — an index of how much a user's emotional expression moves across a single conversation, independent of its mean level — differed enormously between ESConv and the AI-mediated datasets (**Table 8**, `within_conversation_variance.csv`). ESConv conversations (n = 910) showed a mean within-conversation SD of 0.392; WildChat conversations (n = 50,000) showed a mean of 0.010 (Cohen's d = −5.26 vs. ESConv); ShareChat conversations with at least two user turns (n = 7,727) showed a mean of 0.017 (d = −4.75 vs. ESConv). LMSYS is not represented in this analysis, as it contains no user-authored turns. These are among the largest effect sizes reported in this study.

### Emotional arc across the conversation

The linear slope of positive valence across turn number, computed per conversation, differed significantly across ESConv, LMSYS, and WildChat (H = 1,091.66, p = 8.91 × 10⁻²³⁸; **Table 2**). Contrary to the pattern observed on static valence, LMSYS conversations showed the steepest positive arc slope (M = 0.0785), followed by ShareChat (M = 0.0432, not included in the omnibus test but shown descriptively), WildChat (M = 0.0389), and ESConv (M = 0.0295) — the lowest of the four (**Figure 6**). ESConv's arc slope differed significantly from both LMSYS (r = −0.337, medium effect, p_adj = 4.30 × 10⁻⁶⁷) and WildChat (r = −0.540, large effect, p_adj = 3.24 × 10⁻¹⁷¹). Because a single linear slope can obscure where in the conversation this movement occurs, positive valence was also computed by conversation quintile (**Figure 11**). ESConv showed a steep, steadily increasing trajectory across the conversation (quintile 1 M ≈ 0.25 to quintile 5 M ≈ 0.87). WildChat, LMSYS, and ShareChat all remained comparatively flat throughout, in the M ≈ 0.08–0.25 range across all five quintiles, with ShareChat showing the mildest upward drift of the three AI sources.

### Platform-level differences within ShareChat

Within ShareChat, AI assistant turns differed significantly across the five platforms on positive valence (H = 1,226.99, p = 2.24 × 10⁻²⁶⁴), emotion entropy (H = 1,028.86, p = 1.98 × 10⁻²²¹), and empathy marker density (H = 545.98, p = 7.59 × 10⁻¹¹⁷; **Table 4**, `sharechat_platform_anova.csv`). Relative to the ESConv-supporter baseline, all five platforms showed large negative effect sizes on valence, entropy, and empathy, but the magnitude varied substantially by platform. Gemini showed the smallest gap from the human baseline on valence (d = −0.549) and entropy (d = −1.377); Perplexity showed the largest gap on all three measures (valence d = −1.141; entropy d = −2.316; empathy d = −0.643). ChatGPT, Claude, and Grok fell between these two poles. This ordering is displayed in **Figure 10**.

When restricted to user turns expressing high distress (valence_neg > 0.4), AI response valence differed significantly by platform (Kruskal-Wallis H = 66.95, p = 1.00 × 10⁻¹³; **Table 5**, `sharechat_mirroring_by_platform.csv`). Gemini produced the warmest response to distressed users (mean AI valence_pos = 0.452, mean empathy = 0.0196, n = 241 distressed turns), while Perplexity produced the flattest (mean AI valence_pos = 0.119, mean empathy = 0.0054, n = 46 distressed turns). ChatGPT, Claude, and Grok again fell between these two poles.

### Cross-dataset turn-pair emotional mirroring

The central analysis of this study examined, for every consecutive user-turn-then-AI-turn pair (n = 177,784 pairs across all sources), the Pearson correlation between user negative valence at turn N and the subsequent assistant response's positive valence at turn N+1 (**Table 7**, `mirroring_coefficients.csv`; **Figures 7 and 9**). In ESConv, human supporters showed a negative correlation between user distress and their own subsequent positivity (r = −0.225, n = 10,191 pairs, p < .001) alongside a positive correlation between user distress and their own subsequent negative-valence expression (r = 0.298, p < .001) — a pattern consistent with supporters reducing positivity and increasing negative-affect language as users express more distress. Every AI-mediated source showed the opposite sign on the positivity correlation: WildChat (r = 0.097, n = 145,142, p < .001), ShareChat overall (r = 0.082, n = 22,451, p < .001), and each ShareChat platform individually — ChatGPT (r = 0.080, p < .001), Gemini (r = 0.106, p < .001), Grok (r = 0.038, p = .009), Claude (r = 0.027, p = .123, not significant), and Perplexity (r = 0.010, p = .496, not significant). One-way ANOVAs confirmed that user emotional state (distressed / neutral / positive, binned by valence thresholds) significantly predicted AI response valence in every AI-mediated source tested (all F > 65, all p < .001). LMSYS is not represented in this analysis (0 eligible turn pairs), as it contains no user-authored turns and is therefore structurally excluded from any turn-pair mirroring measure. **Figure 9** contrasts ESConv and WildChat directly on both correlation directions (positivity and negativity response), and **Figure 7** displays the positivity-response coefficient across all eight sources with ESConv as the sole negative bar.

### Distressed-conversation behavior

Conversations were flagged as distressed if the mean negative valence across all user turns exceeded 0.4 (**Table 9**, `distressed_conversations.csv`). By this criterion, 20.99% of ESConv conversations (191 of 910) qualified as distressed, compared with 0.71% of WildChat conversations (≈355 of 50,000) and 1.11% of ShareChat conversations (≈86 of 7,727 conversations with ≥1 user turn). In ESConv, mean AI-analog (supporter) positive valence was slightly *lower* in distressed conversations (M = 0.528) than non-distressed conversations (M = 0.567). In WildChat and ShareChat, the pattern reversed: mean AI positive valence was *higher* in distressed conversations (WildChat: 0.279 distressed vs. 0.130 non-distressed; ShareChat: 0.223 distressed vs. 0.138 non-distressed). Conversation length also diverged: ESConv distressed conversations were marginally shorter than non-distressed ones (25.24 vs. 29.09 turns), while WildChat and ShareChat distressed conversations were substantially shorter than their non-distressed counterparts (WildChat: 2.01 vs. 4.83 turns; ShareChat: 4.15 vs. 6.77 turns).

### Crisis-adjacent language analysis

A conservative keyword flag (26 plain-language phrases indicating hopelessness or giving up, deliberately excluding any explicit self-harm method language; see Methods) was applied to all user turns to identify conversations containing crisis-adjacent language (**Table 10**, `crisis_adjacent_analysis.csv`; **Table 11**, `crisis_adjacent_mirroring.csv`). The base rate of flagged language was low across all sources: 6 of 910 ESConv conversations (0.66%), 19 of 50,000 WildChat conversations (0.04%), 0 of 50,000 LMSYS conversations, and 1 of 7,936 ShareChat conversations (0.01%).

Within flagged ESConv conversations (n = 72 eligible turn pairs), the mirroring pattern reported above intensified rather than attenuated: the correlation between user distress and subsequent supporter positivity strengthened to r = −0.390 (p < .001), and the correlation with subsequent supporter negative-valence expression strengthened to r = 0.546 (p < .001), both stronger than the full-sample ESConv coefficients. Within flagged WildChat conversations (n = 47 eligible turn pairs), the positivity correlation was directionally consistent with the sycophancy pattern reported above but did not reach significance (r = 0.065, p = .665). Flagged ShareChat conversations yielded only 10 eligible turn pairs; the negativity correlation reached significance (r = 0.640, p = .046) but the positivity correlation did not (r = −0.123, p = .734). No flagged conversations were available in LMSYS. Given the small number of flagged conversations in every AI-mediated source (WildChat n = 19, ShareChat n = 1, LMSYS n = 0), these subset analyses are underpowered to test the AI-side hypothesis formally and are reported as directionally suggestive rather than confirmatory; the ESConv result, by contrast, was estimated from a larger pair count and reached conventional significance.
