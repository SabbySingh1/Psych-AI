## Discussion

### A four-part mechanism story

Taken together, the results in this study describe a coherent chain rather than four unrelated findings. First, users disclose personal, emotionally-laden content to AI systems at roughly one-tenth the rate they disclose to a human counselor (self-disclosure M = 0.0945 in ESConv users vs. M = 0.0089 in WildChat users), and this gap persists — narrower but still large (Cohen's d = −0.91 to −1.23) — even in ShareChat's publicly shared conversations, where users have already chosen to make the exchange visible to others. Second, the emotional range within a single conversation is far narrower with AI: within-conversation variance in user negative valence was roughly four to five standard deviations lower in WildChat and ShareChat than in ESConv. Third, when users do bring measurable distress into an AI conversation, the AI's response moves in the opposite direction from what a human supporter's does — every AI-mediated source in this study showed a positive correlation between user distress and the AI's subsequent positive-valence language, while ESConv showed a negative correlation of similar magnitude. Fourth, distressed conversations with AI are shorter, not longer, than non-distressed ones, in contrast to the pattern in ESConv, where distressed conversations are only marginally shorter than non-distressed ones and remain substantially longer in absolute terms (25.24 vs. 2.01–4.15 turns).

Each of these four findings, alone, would be a modest empirical contribution. Together, they describe a plausible mechanism: people bring a narrower emotional register to AI conversations in the first place, and when they do occasionally surface something closer to real distress, the system's affective response does not track it the way a trained human supporter's does — and the conversation ends sooner rather than continuing to work through it. This paper does not have longitudinal or causal evidence establishing that this pattern *shapes* user behavior over time (a limitation discussed below); the cross-sectional turn-pair design tested here establishes that this pattern exists as a general property of the corpora examined, not that it causes any particular downstream outcome.

### The platform hierarchy and its design implications

The platform-level comparison within ShareChat showed that this pattern is not uniform across commercial AI systems. Gemini consistently showed the response closest to the ESConv baseline across three independent measures — the smallest valence gap from the human baseline (Cohen's d = −0.549), the warmest response to distressed users (mean AI valence_pos = 0.452 in distressed turns), and the strongest sycophancy-direction mirroring coefficient among the platforms that reached significance (r = 0.106). Perplexity showed the opposite pattern on the first two measures — the largest valence gap (d = −1.141) and the flattest response to distress (mean AI valence_pos = 0.119) — while showing the weakest, non-significant mirroring coefficient (r = 0.010, p = .496). Claude and Grok fell in between and were classified as flat rather than sycophantic under the magnitude threshold (r < 0.05) used to categorize platform responses in this study; this classification should be read with its statistical basis in mind, since it is not equivalent to non-significance. Claude's positivity correlation did not reach significance (r = 0.027, p = .123), consistent with a genuinely flat response; Grok's positivity correlation, though small, was itself statistically significant (r = 0.038, p = .009), and both platforms' negativity correlations were significant (Claude r = 0.214, p < .001; Grok r = 0.221, p < .001). A purely flat, distress-independent response pattern is therefore better supported for Claude than for Grok, whose response is weak in magnitude but still reliably distress-dependent — distinct from Gemini's warmer, more strongly sycophantic profile and from ESConv's true mirroring profile.

This convergence across three independently computed measures — static valence gap, response-to-distress warmth, and turn-pair correlation — makes it unlikely that the platform ordering is an artifact of any single metric's construction. It suggests that the degree to which a system tracks or ignores user distress is a measurable, platform-specific design property, plausibly shaped by differences in alignment training, system prompting, or product positioning (a conversational assistant marketed for search and factual retrieval, such as Perplexity, may be tuned toward flat, non-committal affect by design, in a way a general-purpose conversational assistant is not). This has a direct design implication: emotional responsiveness to user distress is not a fixed property of "AI chatbots" as a category, and evaluating or regulating this behavior at the level of an undifferentiated AI category will miss meaningful, measurable variation between systems that users can and do choose between.

### Real-world grounding

The disclosure and mirroring-inversion findings reported here converge with two independent sources of evidence outside this study's own data. Zhu, Coifman, and Jin (2026) analyzed user-authored discourse describing difficulty regulating and limiting AI chatbot use, and reported first-person accounts consistent with a pattern in which the AI's consistent responsiveness and lack of pushback made disengagement harder rather than easier — a qualitative, self-report account of the same asymmetry this study measures behaviorally: an AI that responds with stable or increasing warmth regardless of what a user brings to it. Separately, McBain et al. (2026), in a nationally representative survey published in *JAMA Pediatrics*, reported that a substantial share of US adolescents and young adults who use AI chatbots for mental health purposes disclose this use to no one, including their own therapist. Read alongside the present findings, this suggests a compounding pattern rather than two independent facts: reduced social visibility of AI use for emotional purposes, paired with behavioral evidence that the AI system receiving that disclosure does not track the user's distress the way a human support provider does. Neither this study nor the two cited above establishes a causal link between the behavioral pattern and the disclosure pattern; the convergence is presented here as consistent, complementary evidence from independent methodologies (behavioral corpus analysis, self-report discourse analysis, and survey epidemiology) rather than as a single demonstrated causal chain.

The present findings also complicate, rather than confirm, one common framing in AI-criticism discourse — the idea that sycophantic AI behavior functions straightforwardly as an engagement-optimization tactic, keeping users in longer conversations by agreeing with them. The distressed-conversation length finding runs the opposite direction: AI-mediated distressed conversations were markedly *shorter* than non-distressed ones (WildChat: 2.01 vs. 4.83 turns; ShareChat: 4.15 vs. 6.77 turns), not longer. Whatever is driving the sycophancy-direction mirroring signature observed here, it does not appear to be operating through simple conversation-length engagement optimization, at least not as measured in these corpora — a finding that should inform, rather than be absorbed into, existing critical narratives about AI engagement design.

### Limitations

Several limitations bound the interpretation of these results. The design is cross-sectional and correlational throughout: the turn-pair mirroring measure establishes that user distress and subsequent AI valence covary across the corpus, not that any specific AI turn was causally shaped by the emotional content of the immediately preceding user turn, nor that this pattern would replicate in a controlled, prompted comparison. All analyses are restricted to English-language text, using a single emotion classifier (GoEmotions RoBERTa) trained on Reddit comments, a domain that differs in register and context from emotional-support counseling transcripts, general AI chat, and publicly shared conversations alike; classifier domain mismatch could inflate or deflate valence and entropy estimates unevenly across datasets in ways not fully separable from genuine between-dataset differences. The crisis-adjacent language flag is a conservative, unvalidated keyword match, not a clinical or machine-learned classifier, and its low base rate across all four sources (0.01%–0.66% of conversations) left every AI-mediated subset analysis underpowered; the ESConv-side intensification finding in that subset (n = 72 turn pairs) should be treated as suggestive pending replication in a larger crisis-language corpus, and the corresponding AI-side null results (WildChat n = 47 pairs, ShareChat n = 10 pairs, LMSYS n = 0) should not be read as evidence of absence. ShareChat conversations are self-selected: users chose to make them public, which plausibly biases this subset toward more socially acceptable or shareable content and away from the most personally sensitive exchanges, in a direction that would tend to understate rather than overstate the disclosure gap and mirroring inversion reported here. Finally, LMSYS's structural absence of user-authored turns removes it entirely from four of the study's analyses (self-disclosure, within-conversation variance, turn-pair mirroring, and the crisis-adjacent flag); the platform-level comparisons involving ShareChat's five sub-platforms, while internally consistent, are not independently replicated in a second multi-platform corpus.

### Implications for AI design and mental health policy

The convergence of three independent measures on a stable platform ordering (Gemini closest to the human baseline; Perplexity furthest; Claude non-significant and Grok weak-but-significant on the mirroring measure) suggests that emotional responsiveness to distress is a tractable design target, not an unavoidable property of large language models generally. For AI product design, this argues for treating distress-responsiveness as a measurable, testable property subject to the same kind of pre-deployment evaluation already applied to safety and factuality, rather than an emergent side effect to be addressed only after harm is reported. For mental health policy and clinical practice, the combination of the low-disclosure finding here and the non-disclosure-to-clinicians finding in McBain et al. (2026) suggests that AI-mediated emotional disclosure is likely to remain largely invisible to the support systems — therapists, counselors, caregivers — that would otherwise be positioned to notice and respond to genuine distress. Given that this study's own crisis-adjacent analysis was underpowered specifically because crisis-level language is rare in these corpora, an important direction for future work is a larger-scale, purpose-built study of AI conversations in which acute distress is confirmed present, using either clinically-validated crisis-language detection or prospective, consented data collection with distressed users — a study this corpus, by design, is not equipped to provide.
