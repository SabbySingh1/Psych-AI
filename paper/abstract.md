## Abstract

AI companion platforms respond with unconditional validation, but this has largely been studied within single apps, without a human baseline. Using a unified emotion-classification pipeline applied to four large-scale corpora — ESConv (human counseling, n = 910 conversations), WildChat and LMSYS-Chat-1M (naturalistic and multi-model AI chat, n = 100,000 combined), and ShareChat (public conversations across five commercial platforms, n = 7,936) — this study tested whether AI tracks user distress as human supporters do, and whether this varies by platform. Four converging findings emerged. Users disclosed personal content to AI at roughly one-tenth the rate observed with human counselors (≈10.6×), a gap persisting even in publicly shared AI conversations (d = −0.91 to −1.23). Emotional range within a single AI conversation was four to five standard deviations narrower than with a human counselor. Turn-pair analysis of 177,784 user-to-AI exchanges showed human supporters reduced their own positivity as user distress rose (r = −0.225), while every AI platform showed the opposite direction (r = 0.010–0.106) — a sign inversion, not attenuation. This varied by platform: Gemini's response most closely approximated human tracking; Perplexity's was flattest; Claude's response was flat and non-significant; Grok's was weak but statistically sycophantic. AI-mediated distressed conversations were also markedly shorter than non-distressed ones, complicating engagement-optimization accounts of this behavior. These findings extend recent work on AI companion accommodation (Chu et al., 2025, 2026) beyond single platforms to a systematic, human-baselined, cross-platform comparison, and converge with independent survey and discourse evidence on AI mental-health disclosure and dependency.
